# Training Principles

Use these principles as decision rules when designing resistance training programs.

## Specificity

Training should support the desired adaptation and sport demand. Consider:
- Muscles and joints involved
- Movement pattern and plane
- Muscle action: concentric, eccentric, isometric
- Velocity and force demands
- Coordination pattern
- Season and competition context

Specificity does not mean mimicking sport skills under load. Choose exercises that transfer physical qualities without distorting sport technique.

## Overload

The training stimulus must exceed what the athlete is accustomed to. Overload may come from:
- Higher load
- More sets or repetitions
- More sessions
- More difficult exercise variation
- Shorter rest
- Higher movement velocity or intent
- More complex execution

Overload can also involve strategic reduction, such as deloading volume so accumulated fitness can be expressed.

## Progression

Progression is the planned increase or refinement of training stress. It should be gradual and tied to:
- Training status
- Exercise technique
- Current performance
- Recovery
- Season
- Primary training outcome

Common progressions include load, volume, exercise complexity, range of motion, velocity intent, density, and weekly frequency.

## Variation

Variation reduces monotony and gives the athlete novel but relevant stimuli. Use variation at:
- Macro level: different emphases across blocks
- Micro level: heavy/light days, exercise rotation, set configuration, rest changes

Variation should serve the training goal. Random exercise changes are not periodization.

## Reversibility

Qualities decline when the training stimulus is removed or insufficient. Maintain de-emphasized qualities with retaining loads during blocks that target another quality.

## Individualization

Programs should reflect the athlete's sport, position, training status, goals, injury history, and response to training. Exercises may overlap across athletes, but load, volume, progression, and constraints should be individualized.

## Emphasis And De-Emphasis

Most blocks should have one primary quality. Secondary qualities may be maintained with lower volume. This is especially useful in team sports where many qualities matter at once.

## Fatigue Management

Account for all stressors:
- Strength training
- Sport practice
- Competition
- Travel
- Sleep
- Academic/work stress
- Psychological stress
- Nutrition and hydration

If performance, mood, sleep, or soreness worsens across multiple sessions, reduce volume or intensity before adding more work.
